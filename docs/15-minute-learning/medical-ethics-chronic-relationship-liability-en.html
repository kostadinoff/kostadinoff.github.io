Set aside about 15 minutes to read and think. The text follows the presentation “Physician, patient, team and law”. Afterwards you should be able to explain why chronic illness calls for a different relationship from acute illness, recognise the models of the physician–patient relationship, say where collegiality ends, and distinguish the kinds of legal liability a physician can face in Bulgaria.

Chronic illness as a long-term human condition

Acute medicine is organised around a clear onset, a diagnosis, an intervention and the end of an episode of care. In chronic illness the diagnosis does not close the problem; it opens a long relationship. Medication, measurements, diet and rehabilitation happen mostly at home. The outcome therefore depends not only on the right prescription but on whether the plan is understandable, acceptable and workable in this person’s life.

The distinction between disease and illness helps. Disease is described by a diagnosis and measurements. Illness is how the person lives through the symptoms, uncertainty, treatment and changed roles. Two people with the same diagnosis may struggle with quite different things: one loses their job, the other fears dependence. The ethical problem arises when the logic of acute care is carried mechanically into long-term care and the patient becomes a passive recipient of instructions.

Coping with illness

Coping is the effort to manage demands that exceed a person’s usual resources. Problem-focused strategies seek information, change routines and organise help. Emotion-focused strategies reduce distress through distancing, sharing feelings or religious support. Meaning-focused strategies reorder goals and find values beyond the illness. No strategy is good or bad in itself. Brief denial can prevent collapse in the first days; prolonged denial delays treatment.

A diagnosis often splits life into “before” and “after”. Fear, anger, denial and sadness follow no fixed order and come back with each new complication. Anger at the team is often anger at the illness. Hopelessness (“there is no point in treatment”) calls for assessment of uncontrolled pain, depression and self-harm risk; it should not simply be taken as a final refusal. Adaptation is not resignation. The physician supports it by acknowledging losses without reducing the person to them.

Autonomy, adherence and treatment burden

Chronic illness increases dependence on drugs, machines, relatives and institutions. Dependence does not remove autonomy: a patient on haemodialysis may be fully able to set their own priorities. Relational autonomy reminds us that choices take shape in relationships, so the physician checks whether a decision expresses a free preference or guilt, fear and pressure.

Adherence has replaced the older term “compliance”. It describes behaviour that matches a plan agreed with the patient. Non-adherence may be unintentional (missed doses, a complex regimen, cost, no pharmacy nearby) or intentional (side effects, fear of dependence, a different view of benefit). Look for the cause before looking for someone to blame. Treatment burden is the work that treatment places on the patient: taking drugs, measuring, attending appointments, travelling, paying. With multimorbidity, separate guidelines can add up to an unworkable plan, and the question becomes “what can this person sustain?” as well as “what else can we prescribe?”.

The family brings care and knowledge of daily life but does not own the decisions. Kinship gives no right to information without the patient’s consent. Overprotection erodes skills, and an exhausted relative is not necessarily irresponsible. When neglect or abuse is suspected, the patient is heard alone.

Models of the physician–patient relationship

Models are analytical tools, not labels for a physician’s character. One consultation can contain elements of several.

The international literature uses three classic classifications, and each answers a different question. Szasz and Hollender ask how far the patient can take part, given his clinical condition. Veatch asks who holds ethical authority in the relationship. Emanuel and Emanuel ask how the physician deals with the patient’s values and what autonomy means. Because the criteria differ, the models of different authors do not correspond one-to-one.

Szasz and Hollender (1956) distinguish three degrees of participation according to the patient’s condition. In activity–passivity the patient cannot take part: resuscitation, coma, general anaesthesia. In guidance–cooperation the physician leads and the patient knowingly follows: an acute illness with a clear standard of treatment. In mutual participation tasks and decisions are shared: chronic illness, rehabilitation, behaviour change. The error is to keep the patient passive after capacity has returned.

Emanuel and Emanuel (1992) describe four models based on how the patient’s values are understood. In the paternalistic model the physician is a guardian who decides what is best. In the informative model the physician is a technical expert who supplies facts and carries out the patient’s choice. In the interpretive model the physician is a counsellor who helps the patient clarify unclear or conflicting goals. In the deliberative model the physician is a teacher or friend who argues for health-related values without moralising. The informative model can leave the patient alone with a hard choice, and the authors favour the deliberative model as the general ideal.

Veatch (1972) describes the engineering, priestly, collegial and contractual models. The engineering model reduces the physician to a scientist handing over facts and leaves values out of the conversation. The priestly model is paternalism from a position of moral authority. The collegial model assumes two equals with a shared goal, though equality and shared interests are often missing. The contractual model, which Veatch favours, shares ethical authority: physician and patient agree a framework of values and obligations while each keeps his moral integrity. Its risk is minimalism: “only what was agreed”. It is an ethical model of the relationship and does not by itself create contractual civil liability, which has its own legal conditions.

Strong paternalism restricts a capable, informed patient and is not justified. Weak paternalism protects a person whose capacity for the specific decision is impaired, and lasts only while that ground exists. A patient may refuse offered treatment but cannot demand harmful or needless treatment. The Bulgarian Code of Professional Ethics lets a physician decline treatment when trust is absent (Art. 12), but never emergency care (Art. 11).

Shared decision-making combines the professional recommendation with the patient’s values. Elwyn and colleagues (2012) describe three steps: choice talk (“there is a choice, and we will make it together”), option talk (the reasonable options with their benefits, harms and uncertainty) and decision talk (“what matters most to you?”). In practice: acknowledge that there is a choice; present the reasonable options, including waiting or refusal; clarify what matters to the patient; give a reasoned recommendation; agree a plan and check understanding. The Bulgarian Health Act guarantees the right to clear, accessible information and to more than one medical opinion (Art. 86(1)), and gives the physician freedom of action and decision according to qualification, medical standards and ethics (Art. 190(1)).

Truth-telling and professional boundaries

The treating physician must inform the patient about his state of health, the disease and its prognosis, the planned activities and their risks, and the alternatives (Health Act, Art. 92(1)). Only the patient may decline this information — the refusal is recorded in writing in the medical record (Art. 92(2)–(3)) — or authorise, in writing, another person to be informed instead of him (Art. 92(4)). A family’s request to conceal a diagnosis is explored with respect, but it does not replace the patient’s own wish. Protocols such as SPIKES (setting, perception, invitation, knowledge, emotions, strategy and summary) are memory aids for breaking bad news, not scripts.

Professional boundaries keep the relationship directed towards the patient’s care. A boundary crossing — a modest customary gift shared with the team, attending a funeral — may be acceptable. A boundary violation exploits the relationship: an expensive, secret or conditional gift, a romantic relationship with a patient, informal prescribing for relatives. The Code of Professional Ethics forbids using an examination or treatment for intimate contact with the patient (Art. 27). A medical certificate must state the medical truth: a physician who knowingly provides someone with a false certificate about his state of health, when not acting as an official, commits an offence (Penal Code, Art. 312(1)); for officials, Art. 311 applies.

Collegiality and teamwork

Section III of the Code governs relations between physicians. Collegiality is a basic duty, observed “while respecting the interests of the patient” (Art. 40). Physicians owe each other moral support, must not slander one another, and do not take their disagreements into public polemics (Art. 41). It is unworthy to discredit a colleague by criticising their treatment or person (Art. 42). This does not forbid reasoned professional criticism through the proper channel: disputes go to the ethics committees (Art. 46).

A consultant reports the result to the treating physician and refrains from judgements in front of the patient (Arts. 36, 45). A medical council (консилиум) is convened when diagnosis or treatment is difficult, and a unanimous decision is binding (Art. 38). Continuity is a moral duty: a physician who withdraws passes on all information (Art. 24(1)), and another physician may change treatment in the treating physician’s absence but must inform them and record the change (Art. 50). Gaps in handover are a common cause of preventable harm.

Nurses, midwives and other professionals have their own competence, professional body and code of ethics. Hierarchy in the team is functional, not personal. An unclear order is clarified, not carried out silently, and any team member must be able to stop a procedure when safety is at risk.

Collegiality does not mean cover-up. A sick colleague has the same rights and the same confidentiality as any patient (Code, Art. 49), but a corridor consultation is not care. When illness or addiction affects a physician’s work, what is needed is individual assessment and proportionate limits, neither concealment nor automatic exclusion. When there is immediate risk, patients are protected through the established procedure and the colleague is offered help.

The physician’s legal liability in Bulgaria: four kinds

This class uses four kinds in this order: disciplinary, administrative, criminal and civil. The disciplinary block groups disciplinary penalties and the employee’s financial liability towards the employer. These have independent legal grounds: compensation for loss is not a disciplinary fine (Labour Code, Art. 203(3)). Professional body liability is an administrative subtype in this teaching scheme; contractual and tort liability are the two civil subtypes.

1. Disciplinary

Basis and consequences. The Labour Code, Arts. 186–188, covers culpable breach of work duties and the penalties of reprimand, warning of dismissal and disciplinary dismissal. The employer or an authorised person imposes them (Art. 192), after hearing the employee or receiving written explanations (Art. 193).

The same block includes employee financial liability towards the employer under Arts. 203 onwards. For negligence, liability is normally capped at the agreed monthly salary; Art. 206(2) sets a different limit for loss caused in managerial duties. Art. 206(3) also covers compensation paid to third parties. For intentional harm or a crime, liability is determined under civil law (Art. 203(2)). Limited financial liability starts with an employer order; if contested in writing, the employer can bring a court claim (Art. 210). Full financial liability is enforced by a court (Art. 211).

Medical example: a physician arrives late for a shift. A disciplinary penalty may follow; separately proven loss to the hospital may also give rise to a duty to compensate it.

2. Administrative, including professional body liability

Basis and consequences. The Administrative Violations and Penalties Act, Arts. 6, 13 and 47, covers administrative violations, penalties including fines, and sanctioning authorities. The relevant special Act defines the specific violation and competent authority. For example, the Health Act, Art. 221, provides for a fine or pecuniary sanction on an establishment that violates patient rights; the Medical Supervision Agency’s executive director issues the penalty decree (Art. 233a). Art. 229 is a residual penalty provision for breaches outside the cases expressly listed there.

Subtype — professional body liability before BLS / BZS. The Professional Organisations of Physicians and Dentists Act, Arts. 37–41, sets out the violations and procedure. For an ethical-code breach, Art. 38 provides for a reprimand, fine or temporary removal from the register. The regional ethics committee hears the complaint (Art. 39), a member records the violation, and the chair of the regional college’s governing board of the Bulgarian Medical Association (BLS) or Bulgarian Dental Association (BZS) issues the penalty decree (Art. 40). Art. 41 applies the administrative penalties procedure. Good-practice compliance is monitored by Medical Supervision jointly with BLS and BZS (Art. 37(2)).

Medical examples: an establishment violates a statutory patient right; a physician discredits a colleague before patients in breach of the ethical code — the professional subtype.

3. Criminal

Basis and consequences. The Criminal Code, Art. 11, addresses intent and negligence. Arts. 123 and 134 concern death or grievous/moderate bodily injury through ignorance or negligent practice of an occupation or regulated activity that is a source of increased danger. All elements of the specific offence, including causation and fault, must be proved. The court decides guilt and imposes punishment; the prosecution brings and maintains the charge in public prosecutions.

Medical example: a negligently administered wrong dose causes death. Art. 123 may apply if all its conditions are proved; the adverse outcome alone does not establish a crime.

Two further offences concern the duty to help. Everyone must help a person in immediate danger to life when he can do so without danger to himself or others (Art. 139). A person practising a medical occupation who, having been called, does not help a patient or a woman in labour without a valid reason commits a special offence (Art. 141).

4. Civil — contractual and tort

The purpose is compensation for damage. The court awards damages on the injured party’s claim if the conditions are established. The Obligations and Contracts Act governs the two subtypes:

  • Contractual: Arts. 79–82 — failure to perform a contractual duty properly; Art. 82 covers direct, immediate and foreseeable losses, or all direct and immediate losses in bad faith. Example: an agreed investigation is not performed and the patient incurs proven additional costs. This does not mean every treatment contract guarantees a cure.
  • Tort / non-contractual: Art. 45 — culpably caused harm, with fault presumed until proved otherwise; Art. 49 — liability of the establishment as principal for harm in or in connection with assigned work. Example: an incorrectly given injection injures a patient’s nerve.

The European Court of Human Rights

The Convention does not guarantee a favourable outcome of treatment, but it requires effective proceedings when something goes wrong. For unintentional harm in health care, an effective civil remedy may be sufficient; criminal proceedings are not required in every case (Calvelli and Ciglio v Italy [GC], 2002). After a death in hospital the State must provide an effective and prompt judicial system to establish the cause and responsibility (Šilih v Slovenia [GC], 2009). Where an effective regulatory framework exists, ordinary negligence does not in itself breach the right to life; the State answers for medical error only in exceptional cases, such as knowingly denying life-saving emergency care or a systemic dysfunction, and under strict conditions (Lopes de Sousa Fernandes v Portugal [GC], 2017). In Vasileva v Bulgaria (2016), a case from Plovdiv, the Court found no violation but restated that proceedings in medical negligence cases need formally and actually independent experts whose reasoned conclusions the courts scrutinise.

One act can engage more than one of the four kinds, subject to the law (Professional Organisations Act, Art. 42(1); Labour Code, Arts. 186 and 203(3)). All sanctions do not follow automatically. In the handover case, assess the physician’s conduct, the colleague and the establishment separately.

Before you open the student tasks, try to answer in three sentences: which relationship model suits a patient with hypertension who takes the tablets irregularly; when collegiality must give way; and which kinds of liability may arise if a new symptom is left out of a shift handover.
